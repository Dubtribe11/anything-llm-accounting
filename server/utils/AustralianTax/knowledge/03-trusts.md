# Trusts

## The basic mechanics (Division 6 ITAA 1936)

A trust is not a separate legal entity and is generally not a taxpayer. Instead:

1. Work out the trust's **net income under s 95** — assessable income less allowable deductions, calculated as if the trustee were a resident taxpayer.
2. Identify who is **presently entitled** to the trust's *distributable income* at midnight on 30 June.
3. Each beneficiary is assessed on their **proportionate share** of the s 95 net income (s 97).
4. The trustee is assessed under **s 98** on the share of a beneficiary under a legal disability or a non-resident, and under **s 99A at 45%** on any income to which no beneficiary is presently entitled.

The **proportionate approach** confirmed in *Bamford* means a beneficiary's share of taxable net income follows their **percentage share of distributable income**, not a dollar amount. When trust (accounting) income and tax net income differ — say because of franking credit gross-ups or a non-deductible expense — a beneficiary entitled to "50% of the income" is assessed on 50% of the *tax* net income, which can exceed the cash they receive.

## The 30 June resolution

- A valid resolution making beneficiaries presently entitled must be made **by 30 June** (or an earlier date if the deed requires it).
- It must be **in writing** and be capable of being evidenced. Minutes signed after year end recording a decision actually made by 30 June are acceptable; a decision made after 30 June is not.
- It must be **within the powers of the deed** and to beneficiaries **within the class of eligible beneficiaries**.
- Check the deed's definition of "income" — an income equalisation clause, a s 95 definition clause, or a power to re-characterise capital as income all change the answer.

Failure to make a valid resolution means the trustee is assessed at 45% under s 99A.

## Streaming

Two categories of income can be **streamed** to particular beneficiaries so they retain their character:

- **Franked distributions** (Subdiv 207-B) — the beneficiary is "specifically entitled" and receives the franking credit with the dividend. The entitlement must be recorded by **31 August** after year end.
- **Capital gains** (Subdiv 115-C) — recorded by **30 June**. The beneficiary grosses the gain back up, applies their own capital losses, then applies their own discount percentage. This is why a company beneficiary specifically entitled to a discount capital gain loses the benefit of the discount.

All other income (interest, rent, business profits, foreign income) **cannot be streamed** — it flows proportionately.

## Minors — Division 6AA

A beneficiary under 18 at 30 June pays penalty rates on trust distributions (unearned income):

| Eligible income | Tax |
| --- | --- |
| $0 – $416 | Nil |
| $417 – $1,307 | 66% of the excess over $416 |
| $1,308+ | **45% of the entire amount** |

Excepted persons (working full time, disabled, entitled to certain pensions) and excepted income (employment income, a deceased estate, damages for injury, property transferred from a deceased estate or family breakdown) are taxed at ordinary rates. In practice the effective limit for distributing to a minor is $416.

## Section 100A — reimbursement agreements

Where a beneficiary is made presently entitled under an agreement in which someone **other than the beneficiary** actually benefits, and a purpose of the agreement is to reduce tax, s 100A treats the entitlement as never having existed — the trustee is assessed at 45%, with **no time limit** on amendment.

The exception for "ordinary family or commercial dealing" is the battleground. See **TR 2022/4** and **PCG 2022/2** risk zones:
- **White zone** — arrangements entered into before 1 July 2014 that the ATO will not review.
- **Green zone** — low risk: the beneficiary actually receives and controls the funds; a distribution to a parent of a minor to pay for the minor's expenses; a distribution retained by the trust where the beneficiary has an unpaid entitlement recorded and can call for payment.
- **Red zone** — high risk: entitlements returned to the controller, circular arrangements, distributions to loss entities or low-rate beneficiaries who never see the money.

## Unpaid present entitlements and Division 7A

A UPE owed by a trust to a **corporate beneficiary** can be a Division 7A loan (TD 2022/11). Practical options:
- pay the entitlement in cash before the company's lodgment day, or
- put it on a complying Division 7A loan with a written agreement, benchmark interest and minimum yearly repayments, or
- hold the funds on a proper sub-trust for the sole benefit of the corporate beneficiary (the ATO's earlier PS LA 2010/4 investment options have run their course — verify the current position).

## Trust losses

Trust losses are **trapped in the trust** and cannot be distributed. To recoup them the trust must pass the tests in **Schedule 2F ITAA 1936**:
- the 50% stake test (fixed trusts),
- the pattern of distributions test,
- the control test,
- the income injection test, and
- for non-fixed trusts, the **50% stake / control** tests.

A **family trust election (FTE)** simplifies this — an FTE trust only needs to pass the income injection test. The cost is that any distribution **outside the family group** attracts **family trust distribution tax at 47%**. Interposed entity elections extend the group. An FTE can generally only be revoked in limited circumstances, so specify the test individual carefully.

## Types of trust

| Type | Characteristics |
| --- | --- |
| **Discretionary (family) trust** | Trustee chooses beneficiaries and amounts each year. Best flexibility for income splitting; the 50% CGT discount flows through. Most common small business/investment structure. |
| **Unit trust** | Fixed entitlements by units. Used for unrelated parties investing together. Fixed trust status matters for losses and franking credits. |
| **Hybrid trust** | Mixture of units and discretionary elements. Attracts ATO scrutiny, particularly for negative gearing. |
| **Testamentary trust** | Created by a will. Minors receiving income from a testamentary trust are taxed at **ordinary adult rates** (excepted trust income), which makes them very effective for estate planning. |
| **Bare trust** | Used for SMSF limited recourse borrowing arrangements. |
| **Deceased estate** | Taxed at individual rates with a graduated scale for the first three years of administration. |

## Foreign beneficiaries and land tax surcharges

Most states impose **foreign purchaser duty and land tax surcharges** where a discretionary trust deed does not irrevocably exclude foreign persons from the beneficiary class. Many deeds have been amended for this — check before acquiring property in NSW, VIC or QLD in particular.

## Trustee checklist before 30 June

1. Estimate the trust's net income and confirm which components are franked distributions or capital gains.
2. Confirm the deed's income definition and the trustee's powers, including streaming powers.
3. Confirm the beneficiary class and any FTE family group boundaries.
4. Model distributions across beneficiaries at their marginal rates, including any bucket company.
5. Consider s 100A risk — will the beneficiary actually receive the benefit?
6. Sign and date the resolution before 30 June and keep it with the trust records.
7. Plan for UPEs to corporate beneficiaries before the company's lodgment day.
